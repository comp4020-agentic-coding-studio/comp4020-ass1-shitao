一畫 — yīhuà — the single brushstroke

In 1710 the monk-painter Shitao wrote that a single brushstroke "defines even that which lies beyond the borders of the universe" — that a thousand hills, ten thousand valleys, a figure, a stand of bamboo, all begin and finish with one continuous line. Draw one below and feel why he thought so.

What the stroke is doing

Shitao's treatise, usually translated as Enlightening Remarks on Painting (畫語錄), makes yihua — "the single brushstroke," sometimes rendered "the holistic brushstroke" — the root every later chapter builds from. It works on two levels at once.

The practical level is exactly what you just felt: a complete design begins and finishes with one stroke, and its character — dry or saturated, swift or measured — is set by nothing but how the hand moves, not by a separate technique bolted on afterward.

The metaphysical level is the claim underneath it: that "myriad strokes are reunited in oneness" through the painter's hand and mind, and that this single, primal line is where painting joins the painter's own nature to the nature of things. Shitao's contemporaries were absorbed in copying the perfected "methods" of past masters. He answered them with what he called no-method — a method built from the brushstroke itself, before any rule exists to imitate.

The method of no-method

"无法而法, 乃为至法" — the method of no method is the perfect method. Shitao didn't mean painting without discipline; he meant that discipline copied from someone else's rulebook stands between you and the stroke, and the stroke is the only teacher that was ever necessary. Every mark you drew above already knew how to be fast or slow, dry or drowned in ink — you didn't consult a rule to make it so.

Try it once more: draw a full circle without lifting the brush, then draw your name as fast as you can. Same tool, same hand, two completely different strokes — and no rule told either one what to be.
